## Final project summary
### Opportunity / problem
The final project addressed the difficulty that left-handed students face when writing on whiteboards in the EngSci common room. Their hand trails over fresh ink, which can:
- smudge or erase writing
- transfer marker residue to the hand
- reduce legibility
- force awkward wrist posture
- create discomfort during writing

### Working environment
The working environment was the **EngSci common room whiteboards**, markers, erasers, and marker trays. The whiteboards were treated as a fixed part of the environment rather than something to be replaced.

### Stakeholders
Stakeholders evidenced across the brief, rescoped brief, and report include:
- **Left-handed EngSci students** as primary stakeholders
- **Right-handed students writing leftward scripts** such as Arabic as similar-use stakeholders
- **Audiences / peers / group members** who need legible writing
- **Facility custodians / facilities / equipment regulators** who care about maintenance, staining, and damage
- **The design team**

### Final recommended concept
The final recommended concept in the Design Report was a **roller support wrist cuff**.

The report describes it as a wrist/arm support with:
- a wrist cuff
- an arm support platform
- a **polystyrene sphere**
- a **ball transfer unit (BTU)**

The concept was intended to support the user’s arm while allowing writing without major smudging or ergonomic strain.

### Main alternatives considered
The final project compared the roller support against at least these alternatives:
- **Dummy marker**
- **Air cushion**
- **Platform**

### Converged outcome
The final report concludes that the **roller support** was the best concept overall because it best balanced:
- writing speed
- low smudging
- ergonomic wrist posture
- acceptable weight

## What the rescoped design brief shows
The rescoped brief is important because it provides direct evidence of how the team improved the inherited framing.

Do **not** describe the team as simply “using” the adopted brief. Describe them as **reframing / rescoping it**.

### Safe rescoping claims supported by the commented PDF
The rescoped PDF shows that the team:
- made the need more specific to left-handed whiteboard users in the EngSci common room
- refined grip-style accommodation by expanding from vague tripod / quadrupod language to named grip styles and linking this to a population-coverage claim
- refined marker tray requirements so the design had to fit both tray sizes and remain stable on the tray
- added / clarified visibility and transparency requirements so the device would not obstruct the user’s line of sight
- refined sound requirements so the design would not disturb others in the common room
- narrowed smudging requirements away from broad “surface contact” logic toward the more relevant issue of **contact with marker ink**
- commented on merging or simplifying overlapping requirements when the earlier framing was redundant
- refined the “ink on hand” requirement so it focused on avoiding contact with marker ink rather than treating all whiteboard contact the same

### Especially important rescoping move
A key rescoping move visible in the commented PDF is the shift from:
- **“no accidental contact with the whiteboard surface”**

toward:
- **“no accidental contact with marker ink that causes smudging / visible change”**

This is a strong portfolio point because it shows better engineering framing: the team recognized that the real stakeholder problem was not generic board contact, but **smudging and ink transfer**.

### How to reference the rescoped brief in portfolio writing
Good phrasing:
- “After pivoting to an adopted brief, the team rescoped it to make the opportunity more testable and more tightly connected to the actual stakeholder issue.”
- “The commented rescoped brief shows the team translating a broad brief into more usable requirements.”
- “A major framing improvement was shifting from general whiteboard-surface contact to marker-ink contact as the relevant engineering concern.”

## Final Design Report: strongest evidence-backed details
These details remain safe to use because they are directly documented in the final report.

### Safe high-level framing
A safe summary:
> The team addressed the need for left-handed students to write on whiteboards in the EngSci common room without smudging, visible legibility loss, or ergonomic strain.

### Requirements and evaluation criteria used in the final report
The final report uses measurable engineering criteria such as:
- writing speed should not meaningfully reduce baseline performance
- smudging should remain below a visible color-difference threshold (ΔE*)
- wrist angles should remain within acceptable ergonomic limits
- total design weight should remain below a one-handed tool guideline threshold

These are good details for showing how the team translated stakeholder needs into measurable criteria.

### Three key design decisions explicitly justified in the report
The report clearly explains at least these three decisions:
1. **Use a polystyrene support ball**
   - chosen for low surface energy and low weight
   - intended to reduce ink pickup while keeping the design lightweight
2. **Use a symmetric wrist cuff**
   - chosen to support ambidextrous use and include more stakeholders than only left-handers
3. **Use a velcro strap**
   - chosen to fit more wrist sizes without adding the mass of multiple clasp / button options

### Verification / testing methods explicitly documented
The report includes:
- proxy test for **writing speed**
- proxy test for **smudging** using **ΔE*** / Euclidean difference
- proxy test for **wrist angle ergonomics** using a modified RULA-style assessment
- weight check for concepts
- **pairwise comparison matrix**
- multiple **Pugh charts**

### Reported outcomes
The report states that:
- the **roller support** did not noticeably hinder writing speed and passed the writing-speed requirement
- the **roller** produced smudging low enough to remain below the chosen visible threshold in the test used
- the **roller support** and **dummy marker** passed the ergonomics proxy test, while platform / control failed in the listed table
- the **air cushion** failed the weight requirement and was eliminated

## Writing instructions for the agent
### 1. Treat Praxis I as a process narrative, not just a final object
The writing should show:
- self-framed opportunity stage
- pivot
- rescoping
- divergence
- prototyping
- convergence
- final recommendation

### 2. Keep summary and annotation distinct
**One-page summary should emphasize:**
- project context
- opportunity
- stakeholders
- main concepts considered
- final concept
- main verification / comparison activities
- final recommendation

**Annotation should emphasize:**
- what the project shows about the user’s design approach
- how framing, divergence, convergence, and representation interacted
- what changed because of the pivot and rescoping
- why evidence-based convergence mattered
- what limitations or lessons emerged

### 3. Do not overclaim individual authorship
This was a **team** project.

Use language like:
- “the team developed…”
- “the team rescoped…”
- “the project used…”
- “the report documents…”

Only use first-person singular claims if the user separately confirms them.

### 4. Use the pivot honestly
Do not hide the fact that the team pivoted.

That pivot is useful portfolio material because it shows the team’s ability to:
- adopt another framing critically
- improve it
- carry a project forward through multiple stages

### 5. Use the napping material carefully
The napping brief should be treated as:
- pre-pivot evidence of early Praxis I framing / brainstorming / divergence

Do not let it replace the final whiteboard project in the portfolio.

### 6. Use the rescoped brief as framing evidence, not just background
The commented rescoped brief is one of the strongest pieces of evidence in the whole Praxis I set for showing real framing work.

### 7. Use Alpha to show non-linearity
Alpha now makes it possible to show that:
- the solution space was broad
- the team had multiple diverging tools in play
- the dummy marker looked strongest at one point
- the final recommendation changed later

This is excellent evidence of a real engineering process rather than a linear story.

### 8. Preserve uncertainty where appropriate
Safe to state:
- the team pivoted
- the napping files are only relevant as early process evidence
- the team rescoped the adopted brief
- the Alpha file names diverging tools and concept questions
- the final report documents the final recommendation and testing

Not safe to state without more evidence:
- exactly what the user personally authored
- exact individual contributions
- personal reflective claims not supported elsewhere

## Good portfolio angles / interpretations
### Angle A: engineering framing after a pivot
This project can show that the user values **improving framing rather than accepting briefs passively**. The rescoped brief demonstrates that a received opportunity still needed engineering reinterpretation.

### Angle B: divergence as de-anchoring
Because the Alpha work shows wishing, freewriting, SCAMPER, and multiple concepts, the project can be framed as evidence that the user values **deliberate divergence to disrupt premature attachment**.

### Angle C: convergence changed the outcome
The Alpha notes suggest the dummy marker looked best at one stage, but the final report recommends the roller support. This supports a strong interpretation that the team used **evidence-based convergence rather than simply staying with its initial favourite**.

### Angle D: practical first-year engineering verification
The project is a good example of how first-year teams can still do credible engineering design using:
- proxy tests
- low-fidelity prototypes
- mathematical estimation
- comparison tools

### Angle E: representation supports argument
Because feedback discusses headings, structure, figures, and video guidance, the annotation can credibly connect this project to the idea that **representation is part of engineering reasoning, not just presentation polish**.
